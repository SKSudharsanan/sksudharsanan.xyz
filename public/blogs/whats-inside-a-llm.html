Steps 01 + 02

Like any computer,
a model only
understands numbers.

It does not read words. So the real question is, how does our prompt turn into numbers, and how do those numbers help the model understand the relationship between the words?

That whole job is handled by the first two steps: tokenization and embedding.

01 — Tokenization

So how do we
actually split it?

Two obvious options: split by characters, or split by whole words. Both break down.

By character
Tiny vocabulary, but every word becomes a long guessing game. Too many combinations to predict well.
By whole word
No dataset on earth holds every word that exists. New and rare words simply break it.
By subword chunk the sweet spot
Common words stay whole. Rare words split into reusable pieces the model has seen elsewhere.

3 tokens. Reusable pieces. "un" and "able" show up in thousands of other words.

WordPiece BERT

SentencePiece Llama, T5

02 — Embeddings

Okay, now we have
numbers. But numbers
have no meaning yet.

A token id like 4881 is just a label. It tells the model nothing about what France is, or that it sits anywhere near Paris.

So the next step gives every token a long list of numbers, a vector, that actually carries its meaning. In other words, its semantic relationship to every other word. This is called embedding.

02 — Embeddings

Think of it as giving
each word a feeling.

Not the spelling, the feel. Its tone, its mood, the kind of words it usually hangs around. Words that feel alike end up sitting close together, and words that clash sit far apart.

happyjoyfulcheerful furious

And because it is all numbers now, those feelings can be done as math:

02 — Embeddings · types

But here is the catch.
One word can mean
many things.

Take the word "break". A coffee break, a glass that breaks, and the brake in your car. Same sound, three completely different meanings. That problem is why embeddings come in different flavours.

Static word2vec, GloVe
One fixed vector per word. "break" is always the same, no matter the sentence.
Contextual inside the LLM
The vector shifts with the words around it. A coffee "break" and a glass that "breaks" finally split apart.
Sentence search, RAG
A whole passage squeezed into one vector, so you can match by meaning instead of keywords.

03 — Attention

So who decides which
"break" we actually
mean?

This is where attention comes in. Every word looks at every other word in the prompt and asks, how much do you matter to me right now? The stronger the link, the more it shapes the meaning.

let'stakeacoffeebreak

"break" leans hard on "coffee", so the shatter and brake meanings never even show up.

04 — The MLP

Attention gathers the
clues. The MLP turns
them into an answer.

Attention is the step where each word collects hints from the words around it. But collecting hints is not the same as understanding them.

The MLP is the next step that takes all those hints and works out what they actually add up to. It is also where most of the model's plain facts are stored, like knowing Paris belongs with France.

Stack these two steps dozens of times, and that depth is the "deep" in deep learning.

05 — Training

But wait. Who taught
the model any of this?

Nobody sat down and coded the answers. The model learned everything by reading unimaginable amounts of text and playing one simple game over and over: guess the next word.

Show it "The capital of France is ___", let it guess, then tell it how wrong it was. Do that trillions of times and the guessing slowly turns into knowing.

@sksudharsanan17 / 26
HOW LLMs WORK · 01
05 — Training · gradient descent

It learns the same way
you learn from mistakes.

Every wrong guess gets a score called the loss, just a number for how far off it was. A big miss means a big number. The model's only goal is to make that number smaller.

So after each guess, it quietly adjusts millions of tiny settings inside it, each one a little dial, turning them just enough to be a bit less wrong next time. Do that again and again and the guesses keep getting better. That slow, steady correction is gradient descent.

05 — Training · why data wins

And this is why the data
matters more than the
model itself.

You can read the whole transformer design in an afternoon. It is public. What you cannot copy is the data, how clean it is, how diverse it is, and the compute to train on it.

Feed it clean, varied text and you get a sharp model. Feed it noise and you get a confused one. Scale only amplifies whatever you put in, mistakes included.

The model is the engine. The data is the fuel, and the fuel decides how far it goes.

It takes every raw score and squeezes the whole list into proper percentages that add up to 100. Now you can finally read it as, how likely is each word, really.

Zoom out

Underneath, it is all
matrix multiplication.

Millions of these operations for every single token. CPUs run them roughly in sequence. GPUs run thousands at once.

That parallelism is why AI lives on graphics cards, not on your laptop's CPU.
